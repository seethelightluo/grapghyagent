Abstract Objective: Both albumin and synthetic colloids such as hydroxyethyl starch (HES) solution are used to optimize hemodynamics in the critically ill. The influence of different long-term infusion regimes on platelet function was studied.

Design:Prospective,randomized study.

Setting: Clinical investigation on a university hospital surgical intensive care unit.

Patients: Twenty-eight consecutive trauma patients (injury severity score $>15$ points) and 28 consecutive nontraumatized surgical patients with sepsis.

Interventions: The patients received either $20\%$ human albumin (HA trauma, $n = 14$ ; HA sepsis, $n = 14$ ) or $10\%$ low-molecular-weight HES solution HES 200/0.5 (HES trauma, $n = 14$ ; HES sepsis; $n = 14$ ) for 5 days to maintain central venous pressure and/or pulmonary capillary wedge pressure between 12 and $16\mathrm{mmHg}$ .

Measurements and results: Platelet function was assessed by ag-. gregometry $(=$ turbidimetric technique) using adenosine diphosphate $2.0\mu \mathrm{mol} / 1,$ collagen $4\mu \mathrm{l} / \mathrm{ml}$ ,and epinephrine $25~\mu \mathrm{mol} / 1$ as inductors. Arterial blood was sampled on the day of admission or the day of diagnosis of sepsis $(=$ baseline value) and over the next 5 days. Standard coagulation parameters (antithrom

bin III, fibrinogen, partial thromboplastin time) were also measured. Total use of HES by the 5th day totalled $4870 \pm 990 \mathrm{ml}$ in the trauma and $3260 \pm 790 \mathrm{ml}$ in the sepsis patients (HA trauma: $1850 \pm 380 \mathrm{ml}$ ; HA sepsis: $1790 \pm 400 \mathrm{ml}$ ). Maximum platelet aggregation decreased significantly during the first 2-3 days after baseline in all groups. At the end of the investigation period, platelet aggregation variables had recovered and reached (or even exceeded) baseline values. Within the entire investigation period, the course of platelet aggregation variables did not differ significantly between HA and HES-treated patients irrespective of whether they were trauma or sepsis patients.

Conclusions: Alterations in hemostasis may occur for several reasons in the critically ill. Human albumin is the preferred first-line volume therapy in patients at risk for coagulation disorders. With respect to platelet function, volume replacement with (lower-priced) low-molecular-weight HES solutions can be recommended in this situation without any risk.

# Introduction

Platelet dysfunction may occur in the critically ill secondary to several disorders [1]. For platelet aggregation (receptor-dependent) fibrinogen binding and glycoprotein are necessary. During platelet activation, alpha and dense granules are secreted, altering platelet membrane receptors and, thus, aggregation. A reduction in fibrinogen-binding sites is believed to be an important cause of platelet function deterioration [2, 3], but the exact pathological mechanisms of platelet dysfunction have not yet been precisely defined. Many classes of drugs used routinely in intensive care may affect platelet function, resulting in either hyperaggregating platelets with subsequent risk of (micro-) thrombosis or hypoaggregability with an increased tendency to bleed [4].

Sufficient volume replacement is enormously important in the hemodynamic management of the critically ill intensive care patient [5]. For several years it was believed that volume therapy with synthetic colloids significantly altered coagulation and platelet function [6]. Thus the natural colloid human albumin [HA] was infused in the critically ill patient at risk for coagulation abnormalities including platelet dysfunction [7]. Dextran is no longer the volume therapy of first choice in most centers, and other synthetic colloid preparations, such as hydroxyethyl starch (HES), have gained in importance [8]. New kinds of HES [e.g., low-molecular weight (LMW) HES with a mean MW ranging between 200000 and $270000\mathrm{Da}$ and lower degree of substitution DS] appear to affect plasma- and platelet-related coagulation to a much lesser, even negligible, extent [9, 10]. In most studies, however, the effects of different kinds of volume replacement on platelet function have been studied only for a short period of time or in the noncritically ill. Thus, in the present study, the influence of long-term infusion of two different solutions on platelet function assessed by aggregometry was investigated in critically ill intensive care patients.

# Materials and methods

# Patients

Twenty-eight consecutive patients from our surgical intensive care unit (ICU) suffering from sepsis secondary to major surgical interventions (except for cardiovascular and neurosurgery patients) and 28 consecutive trauma patients (injury severity score $>15$ points) [11] were studied. Sepsis was assessed according to the guidelines of the members of the ACCP/SCCM [12]. Patients with septic shock, severe renal failure requiring hemofiltration, severe liver insufficiency, and with heparin, aspirin, cyclooxygenase inhibitors or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs in their history were not included in the study.

The patients were randomized prospectively into four groups: either $10\%$ HES (mean MW 200000 Da, DS 0.5 HES sepsis: $n = 14$ , HES trauma: $n = 14$ ) or $20\%$ HA (HA sepsis: $n = 14$ , HA trauma: $n = 14$ ) were infused for 5 days to maintain central venous pressure

(CVP) and/or pulmonary capillary wedge pressure between 12 and $16\mathrm{mmHg}$ during ventilation.

Packed red blood cells (PRBCs) were administered when hemoglobin (Hb) was $< 9\mathrm{g / dl}$ (aim $10\mathrm{g / dl}$ ), fresh-frozen plasma (FFP) was given to normalize coagulation (when prothrombin time was $>30$ s and/or the fibrinogen plasma level was $< 2.0\mathrm{g / l}$ ), and platelet concentrates were infused when the platelet count was $< 30\times 10^{9} / \mathrm{l}$ . Crystalloids (Ringer's solution) were additionally given to all patients to compensate for fluid loss by sweating, gastric tubes, and urine output, or as a solvent for drugs (e.g., antibiotics).

All patients received standard heparin (ranging from 125 to $500\mathrm{U / h}$ ) without differences between the subgroups (trauma/sepsis). Low-dose dopamine $(3\mu \mathrm{g}\cdot \mathrm{kg}^{-1}\cdot \mathrm{min}^{-1})$ was given to all patients. Epinephrine was administered when mean arterial blood pressure (MAP) was $< 60\mathrm{mmHg}$ and the cardiac index (CI) was $< 3.01\cdot \mathrm{min}^{-1}\cdot \mathrm{m}^{-2}$ in spite of sufficient volume infusion (target for CI: $5.0 - 6.01\cdot \mathrm{min}^{-1}\cdot \mathrm{m}^{-2}$ ). Norepinephrine was indicated when systemic vascular resistance was $< 600\mathrm{dyn}\cdot \mathrm{s}\cdot \mathrm{cm}^{-5}$ and MAP was $< 60\mathrm{mmHg}$ (target for SVR: $600 - 800\mathrm{dyn}\cdot \mathrm{s}\cdot \mathrm{cm}^{-5}$ ). Antibiotic therapy (cephalosporines, aminoglycosides) was selected according to daily microbiological screening. Management of the patients (ventilation, feeding, volume therapy) was carried out according to the guidelines of modern intensive care therapy by physicians who were not involved in the study and were blinded to the grouping (HES versus HA infusion).

# Measured parameters and data points

Platelet aggregation was measured from arterial blood samples by the turbidimetric method according to Born and Cross [13] using a double-channel APACT aggregometer (Fa. Abor, Ahrensburg, Germany). The platelet count was adjusted to approximately $150 \times 10^{9} / l$ before aggregometry. Aggregation was induced by adenosine diphosphate (ADP; 1.0 and $2.0\mu \mathrm{mol} / l$ ), collagen $(4\mu \mathrm{g} / \mathrm{ml})$ , epinephrine $(25\mu \mathrm{mol} / l)$ , and NaCl (control). Maximum aggregation (\%) was defined as the maximum increase in light transmission following addition of the aggregation agent (read as a percentage increase). The maximum gradient of platelet aggregation (\%/\min) was defined as the maximum increase per minute (read as percentage increase per minute). All measurements were carried out in duplicate by the same investigator, within $1\mathrm{h}$ after blood sampling, who was blinded to the grouping and the patients' disease. In addition, MAP, heart rate, and CVP were monitored continuously in all patients. Hb and standard coagulation parameters (platelet count, fibrinogen and antithrombin III plasma concentrations, and partial thromboplastin time) were measured in the same plasma samples. All measurements were performed on the day of admission to the ICU or on the day of diagnosis of sepsis (baseline values) and during the next 5 days at 120 hours.

# Statistics

Means $\pm$ standard deviations (SDs) were calculated. One- and two-way analyses of variance with repeated measures (ANOVA, followed by the Scheffé test) were used for statistically interpreting differences between the two subgroups (trauma/sepsis). For a more distinct presentation of changes in aggregation, percentage differences of maximum platelet aggregation from baseline values (given as relative $\%$ changes) were also calculated and additionally tested. $p$ values below 0.05 were considered significant.

Survival during the study period was similar in both subgroups, as was late survival (after the 5 days of the investigation period) (Table 1). The total volume given to the four groups is shown in Table 2. The total use of PRBCs (HES trauma: 57 units; HA trauma: 56 units; HES sepsis: 36 units; HA sepsis: 37 units) and FFP (HES trauma: 24 units; HA trauma: 28 units; HES sepsis: 34 units; HA sepsis: 32 units) did not differ significantly within the two subgroups (Table 2). None of the patients received platelet concentrates during the study period.

# Discussion

Critical illness is often associated with disorders in plasmatic-, platelet-, or endothelial-derived coagulation [14]. Thrombocytopenia is often seen in ICU patients [15]. Platelet counts greater than $100000 / \mu l$ are not associated with bleeding if platelet function is normal, but qualitative functional impairment of platelets may contribute significantly to alterations in hemostasis in these patients [15, 16]. There may be several reasons for platelet dysfunction following trauma and sepsis: extensive bleeding, induction of the inflammatory cascade with the release of various mediators, and microcirculation deterioration with subsequent microthrombosis [1, 17]. Platelet abnormalities are characterized by changes in platelet membrane receptors, loss of alpha and beta granules, and a reduction in platelet adhesiveness and aggregability [13, 18]. Because platelet dysfunction can be expressed as decreased adhesion and a diminished sensitivity to triggering agonists such as ADP, collagen and epinephrine, we performed platelet aggregometry according to Born and Cross [13] in the present study. We used HA as the gold standard because it is preferred by several centers for volume therapy in the critically ill and because, in vivo, albumin does not appear to affects coagulation, including platelet loss and function [5, 7, 19].

Although various colloid substitutes (e.g., dextran, gelatin, and HES) have often been compared with albumin for their hemodynamic properties [5, 8], their effects on coagulation and particularly on platelet function have been discussed relatively seldom and mostly controversially. A reduction in VIIIR:RCo may results in reduced binding to platelet membrane receptor glycoproteins GPIb and GPIIb/IIIa, which may subsequently result in decreased platelet adhesion [9].

The effects of different HES preparations have also only been incompletely defined: both normal and abnormal platelet aggregation have been reported [20, 21]. High-MW (HMW) HES (450000 Da) was shown to diminish the concentrations of VIIIR: Ag and VIIIR: RCo more pronouncedly than HES with a lower MW [22]. After addition of HMW HES at high doses, platelets appeared swollen and showed reduced adhesion [22]. MacIntyre et al. [10] showed that infusion of HMW HES in patients undergoing routine orthopedic surgery resulted in a tendency towards decreased platelet aggregation. HES with a lower mean MW (pentastarch; 270000 Da)

was reported to have, overall, fewer negative effects on the coagulation system [23]. In a recent study, infusion of $500\mathrm{ml}$ LMW HES in healthy volunteers significantly reduced factor VIII:C (average reduction: $-18\%$ ), whereas the activity of the fibrinolytic system remained unchanged in comparison to a control group in whom $500\mathrm{ml}5\%$ albumin was infused [24].

The influence of different volume replacement regimes on the different aspects of the coagulation system has mostly been studied for a limited period of time before and after single bolus application [25]. The effects of volume replacement for several days, however, have not been systematically investigated, particularly not in the critically ill. Since the platelet function of trauma patients may react differently to volume replacement than that of septic patients, in the present study, platelet aggregation was separately assessed in trauma and sepsis patients. One main finding was that the course of platelet aggregation within the investigation period did not differ significantly between HES- and albumin-treated patients: in both the trauma and sepsis groups infusion of LMW HES for 5 days produced similar changes in platelet function as found in patients who received albumin for volume therapy.

We can only speculate why platelet aggregation variables were reduced during the first days after treatment: critical illness is known to be associated with an (ongoing) activation of various cascade systems (e.g., the complement system), the release of various cytokines, and endothelial alterations, all of which may, directly or indirectly, affect platelet function. Additionally, all our patients received heparin (administered similarly to both subgroups), which may also have influenced platelet function [26]. Heparin (indirectly) suppresses platelet activation by inhibiting endogenous thrombin, one of the most important platelet activators. On the other hand, heparin significantly augments platelet activation in whole blood exposed to platelet aggregation agonists in vitro [27].

Both maximum platelet aggregation and the maximum gradient of platelet aggregation increased at the end of the investigation period. Whether this is secondary to recovery of previously inhibited platelets or due to the arrival of new, large platelets cannot be definitely concluded from the present results. HES and HA groups showed similar platelet aggregation at the end of the study period.

It was not the aim of the present study to decide whether different regimes of volume therapy influence patients' outcome. Although the survival rate throughout the study period, and afterwards (late survival), was similar in the HES- and HA-treated groups, and the APACHE II score improved only in the HES-treated patients, our patient population was much too small to draw final conclusions. Moreover, it is doubtful that clinical studies demonstrating differences in outcome which are related to fluid therapy will be possible [28].

The most important objective of fluid therapy is to restore or guarantee tissue perfusion. Understanding the physiologic effects of various types of fluid management, including their influence on platelet function, in the critically ill may be essential to be in this context. In the com

parison with HA infusion in the critically ill, LMW HES was found a safe and effective volume substitute, without the risk of relevant adverse effects on platelet aggregation.
